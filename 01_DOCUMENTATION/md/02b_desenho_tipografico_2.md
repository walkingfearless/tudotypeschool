## 1. Form and Counterform

Every letterform is made of two inseparable elements: the **form** (the inked stroke itself) and the **counterform** (the white space inside and around it). A skilled type designer pays equal attention to both.

### Basic reference shapes

The three fundamental geometric shapes — triangle, square, and circle — underlie all letterforms. The letters **A**, **H/O**, and **O** map directly onto these primary forms. Understanding how these shapes define a glyph's outer silhouette and its internal white space is the first step in type design.

When letters are displayed as outlines, the counterforms become immediately visible and their relationship to the positive strokes becomes apparent. Studying letterforms in this way trains the eye to see the negative space as a designed element, not merely an absence of ink.

### Spatial relationships and colour overlay

Placing letterforms in contrasting colours (or as overlapping transparent layers) reveals how adjacent glyphs share and negotiate space. This approach, common in early design exercises, forces the designer to consider the optical "texture" that a word or line of text produces on the page — not just the individual shapes.

> **Key concept:** A well-designed letter is a balance between its positive mass and its negative space. Both sides of the stroke are drawn simultaneously in the designer's mind.

## 2. How to Start Drawing a Typeface

### Begin with reference characters

You do not start by drawing the whole alphabet. Instead, you choose a small set of **reference characters** — letters that encode the fundamental decisions of the design: stroke weight, axis stress, curves, angles, and proportions. These decisions then propagate outward to every other glyph in the font.

**For uppercase:** begin with **H** and **O**.
**For lowercase:** begin with **n**, **o**, and **y**.

Resolving these five characters essentially defines the design system of the entire typeface.

## 3. Uppercase Reference Characters

### H and I — Stroke weight and height

The capital **H** is the single most important uppercase reference letter. It contains two vertical stems, a crossbar, and open counters, and it:

- establishes the **weight of vertical strokes** (stem thickness)
- sets the **cap height** (the top guideline for all flat-topped capitals)
- defines the ratio between the stroke and the counter width

The capital **I** is used alongside H to confirm the stem weight without the distraction of serifs or crossbars. Together, H and I establish the basic structural DNA of the uppercase.

### O — Axis stress, curve modulation, and contrast

The capital **O** encodes the most important decisions about the character of a typeface:

- **Axis stress:** the angle of the imaginary line connecting the thinnest points of the O. A vertical axis produces a more rational, geometric feel; a diagonal axis evokes calligraphic tradition.
- **Stroke modulation:** the degree of contrast between the thick and thin strokes of the O determines the overall contrast level of the entire font.
- **Curve quality:** the subtlety and tension of the O's curve will be repeated in every other round letter (C, G, D, Q, U, etc.).

A vertical red line through the O in analysis diagrams shows the axis orientation.

### E — Horizontal bar widths and the median line

The capital **E** is used to define:

- the **width and weight of horizontal strokes** (arms, crossbars, brackets)
- the position of the **median line** — the invisible horizontal that bisects the capital zone

The middle arm of the E is typically drawn slightly shorter than the top and bottom arms, and placed slightly above the mathematical midpoint. This is an optical correction: if the arms are equal and placed at the true midpoint, the letter appears bottom-heavy (see Optical Compensations, §6).

### V — Angle closure

The capital **V** establishes the **angle at which diagonal strokes meet**, which directly determines the design of A, W, X, K, M, N, Z, and many other letters. The V is also used to study the relationship between the diagonal stroke width and the vertical/horizontal stroke widths. Diagonals typically need to be drawn slightly thinner than verticals to appear the same weight.

## 4. Uppercase Character Analysis

The following characters were systematically deconstructed in the course to show how each contributes unique structural decisions to the design system.

### H — Vertical stems and crossbar

The H is analysed in two parts. First, the two vertical stems in black are compared to a blue silhouette showing the same letter slightly offset — this reveals the inner spacing decisions (counter width). Second, the crossbar is isolated to examine its weight relative to the stems and its precise vertical position (it sits at or just above the optical midpoint of the cap height).

### E — Arm lengths and counterforms

By overlaying a blue E on a black E and progressively revealing each horizontal arm, the designer can see how the three arms create two distinct counter spaces. The upper counter should be slightly smaller than the lower counter — this is the same optical principle seen in B, S, P, and R.

### K — Diagonal join

The K is shown with its diagonal leg and arm highlighted in blue to reveal how two diagonal strokes meet a vertical stem. The angle and weight of both diagonals must be consistent with the V and W angles established earlier.

### V, U, Y — Diagonal family

These letters are analysed together as members of the diagonal family:

- **V:** two diagonals meeting at a point; the inner apex requires a subtle ink trap or corner treatment at small sizes
- **U:** essentially a V with a curved bottom; the transition from straight stem to curve determines the "round-ness" character of the font
- **Y:** combines the V's diagonal pair with a single vertical descending stem; the diagonal pair defines the font's angle system, and the point where they join the stem must be optically smooth

### X — Intersecting diagonals

The X is the most complex diagonal letter. Two crossing diagonals create an optical build-up of ink at their centre. The designer must thin the strokes at the crossing point (ink trap / optical compensation) to prevent a visible dark blotch. Blue highlights on the slide show the top-left and bottom-right strokes versus the bottom-left and top-right, making the crossing anatomy clear.

### O — Circular modulation

The O is shown progressing from a plain monoline circle to one that exhibits full stroke contrast, axis tilt, and optical overshoot at top and bottom (see §6 for details on overshoot). The designer traces how the thick-to-thin transition runs around the curve.

### G — Open counter and spur

The G is among the most design-specific letters in any typeface. It contains a spur (the horizontal bar that closes the open counter) and a curved arm. The slide uses blue, black, and magenta to show three different design zones: the outer curve, the inner counter, and the spur mechanism.

### D — Vertical stem and curve

The D shows how a straight left stem transitions into a single large curve on the right. The weight of the curve at top and bottom compared to its thinnest point (where the axis stress intersects) determines how lively or static the letter appears.

### B — Double counter

The B carries two stacked counters. The upper counter is always drawn smaller than the lower counter, and the crossbar (the horizontal that divides them) sits above the mathematical midpoint — both are optical corrections (see §6).

### S — Counter tension

The S is anatomically the most demanding roman letter. It consists of two counter spaces of opposite orientation, joined by two sets of curves in opposing directions. A blue highlight isolates the upper curve from the lower, revealing how the axis stress running through the S differs from a simple tilt — it bends.

## 5. Lowercase Letters

### Reference characters: o, n, y

**Lowercase o:** Establishes the same parameters as the uppercase O — axis stress, stroke modulation, and curve tension — but at the x-height. The terminal points of the o define the **stroke thickness of hairlines** (*espessura do filete*) and the style of the **terminal** (*terminal / cauda*).

**Lowercase n:** Contains one vertical stem, one arch, and open counters at the bottom. The join between stem and arch is a critical design decision: the angle and weight of the transition (the "shoulder") determines the character of all arched lowercase letters (h, m, r, u). The n also has serifs on its upper left terminal — the serif style here defines the entire serif vocabulary.

**Lowercase y:** Carries two diagonal branches (defining angles, consistent with the uppercase V family) and a **descender**. The y establishes how far descenders drop below the baseline and what their terminal treatment looks like.

### Typographic metrics

From the lowercase letters, five horizontal zones are established:

| Zone | Description |
|---|---|
| **Ascender** | The height to which tall lowercase letters (h, d, l, f, k) reach |
| **Cap height** | The height of flat-topped uppercase letters (H, E, F, I) |
| **X-height** | The height of the body of lowercase letters (x, n, o, a) |
| **Baseline** | The invisible line all letters sit on |
| **Descender** | The depth to which letters (p, q, g, j, y) drop below the baseline |

The letters **x, p, d, b, q** together display all five zones simultaneously and are the canonical set for checking vertical metrics.

### Additional lowercase reference characters

**x (lowercase):** Confirms x-height and diagonal angles.

**p and b (lowercase):** Define the overall proportion of the bowl relative to the stem, establish the shape of arched forms, and confirm the ascender height.

**f and t (lowercase):** Define the **terminal style of ascenders and descenders** — whether the crossbar of the t is straight or curved, whether the top of the f curves left or carries a flat serif, and how the y's descender terminates. Red circles on the slides mark the exact terminal points on the f (top), t (crossbar), and y (descender).

### Uppercase vs. lowercase stroke weight

A critical principle: **uppercase strokes are drawn slightly thicker than lowercase strokes.** If both are drawn to the same physical weight, the uppercase letters will appear lighter — because they are taller, their strokes span a larger visual area and the same physical thickness looks thinner.

Example from the slides: the H has vertical strokes of 24 units and horizontals of 22 units; the lowercase h has stems of 27 units and strokes of 29 units. These numbers differ by just a few units but are essential for the two cases to appear visually equal in weight.

## 6. Optical Compensations

Geometry and perception diverge. Type designers must constantly override mathematically correct constructions with optically correct ones. This section covers the main families of optical compensation.

### 6.1 Squares, circles, and triangles appear different sizes

When a square, a circle, and a triangle are drawn to the same bounding-box height:

- The **square** appears largest.
- The **circle** appears smaller (its sides taper away from the edges of the bounding box).
- The **triangle** appears smaller still.

This has direct consequences for letterform design: **round and triangular letters must overshoot their reference lines** to appear the same height as flat-topped letters.

> Round letters (O, C, S, Q, U, G) extend slightly **above** the cap height line and slightly **below** the baseline.
> Triangular letters (A, V, W) extend slightly **above** the cap height line and slightly **below** the baseline at their apex/vertex.

Without these overshoots, letters like O, A, and V appear shorter than H, E, or F, even when constructed inside an identical bounding box.

### 6.2 Stroke weight hierarchy: vertical > horizontal > diagonal

Three letterforms demonstrate this rule: **O, I, V.**

- The O's vertical stroke: **33 units** (thickest)
- The I's vertical stem: **33 units** (same as vertical in O — this is the baseline weight)
- The O's horizontal (thinnest point): **28 units**
- The V's diagonal stroke: **29 units** (thinner than vertical, thicker than horizontal)

The rule is: **vertical strokes > diagonal strokes > horizontal strokes.**

This is because the eye perceives horizontal strokes as slightly heavier than verticals of the same physical width (they span the full visible width of a stroke). Horizontals must therefore be drawn thinner to appear equal.

### 6.3 Concentric circles and the illusion of perfect O

Two circles drawn with mathematically identical inner and outer radii (a perfect concentric O) appear uneven. The upper arc looks thinner than the lower arc due to the way the eye moves through the letterform.

The solution: the O should have **slightly different horizontal and vertical stroke widths**, following the stress model established by the calligraphic broad-nib pen. On the slides, the correct O has a horizontal stroke of 28 units and a vertical stroke of 35 units (outer). The "imperfect" concentric O has equal 31-unit strokes all around — it looks circular but feels wrong as a letter.

### 6.4 Optical compensation at intersections

At the point where two strokes join (the shoulder of the n, the junction of the arch in h, m, u), ink appears to build up visually, creating a dark blotch. This is especially visible in low-contrast typefaces (sans serifs, monoline designs).

The correction: **slightly thin the strokes at the join point**, pulling a small amount of mass out of the intersection. The two n's on the slide demonstrate this — the left n has a full-weight junction and appears heavier there; the right n has been corrected and the grey value evens out.

### 6.5 Upper vs. lower counterforms

The human eye perceives the **upper half of a letter as larger than the lower half**, even when both are geometrically equal. This means:

- The upper counter of B must be **smaller** than the lower counter
- The upper counter of S is **smaller** than the lower counter
- The crossbar of E and the dividing bar of B are placed **above the true midpoint**

The slide showing two D's side by side demonstrates this with measurements: the upper counter appears to occupy more space than the lower, even when the two halves are constructed identically.

### 6.6 Text comprehension relies on the upper half of letters

Because the tops of letters in the Latin alphabet carry more distinctive shapes than the bottoms, readers identify words primarily through their upper silhouette. The slide demonstrates this by showing words with their upper half masked (harder to read) versus their lower half masked (easier to read).

This is why descenders and small variations in the lower portion of letters are less critical to legibility than the ascenders and the top profiles of the x-height zone.

### 6.7 Serif asymmetry for visual balance

The left and right serifs of letters like I, H, L, and T do not need to be identical. Visual balance can be achieved through **asymmetrical serifs** — the exact angle, length, and curvature on each side may differ slightly to counteract the asymmetry of how letters sit against their neighbours.

### 6.8 Serif length: left shorter than right

The **left-side serif should be slightly shorter than the right-side serif** for baseline serifs on letters like h, n, m, u, i, l. Measurements from the slides show a difference of approximately 76 units (left) versus 84 units (right). This slight asymmetry compensates for the optical flow of reading left to right: the right serif must carry the reader's eye forward into the next letter.

## 7. Spacing: Kerning and Tracking

> "The typographic instrument, whether a computer or a composing rule, works like a loom. The typographer seeks to weave text as homogeneously as possible. In the same way, good type is designed to produce a lively and homogeneous texture, but careless spacing of letters, lines and words can tear that fabric."
> — Robert Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* (2005)

Spacing is not an afterthought — it is part of the design.

### 7.1 Tracking (overall letter spacing)

**Tracking** refers to a uniform increase or decrease in space applied across a range of characters. Its effect is global: it changes the overall density and colour of a block of text.

The slides show three versions of the same passage — in pink (tight tracking), blue (normal tracking), and pink again (loose tracking) — to demonstrate how the same words can appear cramped or airy depending on this single parameter.

### 7.2 Kerning (pair-specific spacing)

**Kerning** adjusts the space between specific pairs of letters. The default sidebearing values built into the font are designed for most combinations, but certain pairs require special attention.

**General rules:**

- **Diagonal + diagonal letters** should be spaced closest together (e.g., AV, AW, TA, VA, WA)
- **Diagonal + round letters** should be spaced slightly closer than diagonal + straight (e.g., Ae, To)
- **Round + straight letters** should be slightly closer than straight + straight (e.g., oc, od)
- **Straight + straight letters** require the most space to avoid cramping (e.g., II, HH)

The word "WAIVE" on the slide illustrates this: the W–A and A–I pairs are tightly kerned (diagonal–diagonal), while I–V needs a different treatment because the V introduces new diagonal tension.

The word "exemplo" is annotated with red bars at specific letter pairs, showing where kerning exceptions apply based on shape geometry.

### 7.3 AW — diagonal pair example

The two letters A and W placed together demonstrate the tightest kerning scenario in the Latin alphabet: two letters composed entirely of diagonals meeting with complementary angles. The slide shows measurement lines above both letters at cap height and baseline, with the pair positioned so that the visual space between them matches the internal white space of each letter — the designer's goal for all spacing.

## 8. Analog Drawing Process

Before any software is opened, a typeface begins by hand.

### Stage 1 — First sketches

Initial drawings are loose, exploratory, and small. The designer tests multiple directions rapidly on paper, capturing the gestural energy of the letters without committing to specific measurements. Reference characters (H, O, n, o) are the first marks made.

### Stage 2 — Refined studies

Selected concepts are drawn at larger scale, with more attention to proportions, stroke widths, and curve tension. Pencil or pen is used to study individual letters in isolation, then in pairs and short words.

### Stage 3 — Technical drawing with construction lines

The most promising direction is drawn with full construction lines: baseline, x-height, cap height, ascender, and descender are ruled in. Stroke widths are measured and marked. Hatching or shading is added to understand visual mass distribution. At this stage the drawings begin to resemble what a scanner or camera can hand off to digital tracing.

### Stage 4 — Reference collection and wall study

Printed specimens, photocopies of historical type, and test prints are pinned to a wall for comparison. The designer steps back to evaluate the whole alphabet at once, assessing consistency, rhythm, and colour. Letters are compared side by side across different scales.

### Stage 5 — Word testing

Full words are assembled — typically text strings designed to expose problem pairs, like "theory", "thnoy", or "minimum" — and evaluated for spacing rhythm, counterform consistency, and overall texture.

## 9. Typographic Baseline System

Every typeface is drawn on a consistent grid of five horizontal reference lines. These lines are defined once and then govern every glyph in the font.

| Line | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Highest | **Ascender line** | Top limit for ascender letters (d, h, k, l) |
| Second | **Cap height** | Top of flat capital letters (H, E, F, I) |
| Middle | **X-height** | Top of the lowercase body (n, o, a, x) |
| Fourth | **Baseline** | The line all letters sit on |
| Lowest | **Descender line** | Bottom limit for descender letters (p, q, g, y, j) |

The letters **p, d, l, b, q** together display all five zones in a single word, making them the standard test string for vertical metrics.

Round letters overshoot the cap height, baseline, and x-height slightly above and below these lines (see §6.1). Flat letters sit precisely on them.

## Key Principles — Quick Reference

| Principle | Rule |
|---|---|
| Start with reference letters | Uppercase: H, O. Lowercase: n, o, y |
| Stroke weight hierarchy | Vertical > diagonal > horizontal |
| Overshoot for round/triangular forms | O, C, G, A, V, W extend beyond the reference lines |
| Upper counter < lower counter | Apply to B, E, S, P, R and lowercase equivalents |
| Diagonal letters kern tighter | AV, AW, TA closer than II, HH |
| Uppercase strokes thicker than lowercase | Same weight looks lighter at larger cap height |
| Intersection compensation | Thin strokes at joins to prevent dark build-up |
| Left serif slightly shorter than right | Supports reading direction (left to right) |
| Perfect geometry ≠ perfect letterform | Always correct mathematically equal forms optically |
| Form and counterform are inseparable | Design both sides of every stroke simultaneously |
